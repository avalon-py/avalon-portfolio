Model Performance Summary

The predictive performance of the proposed macro-sentiment framework was evaluated across four primary financial instruments: S&P 500 Index (SPX), Gold Futures (GOLD), CBOE Volatility Index (VIX), and the 10-Year U.S. Treasury Yield (TNX).

The model was trained using transcribed FED speeches from 1996-2022, and evaluated using dataset from 2023-2025. All look-ahead bias has been meticulously avoided.

Final Conclusion

Overall results indicate that central bank communication embeddings, when integrated with macroeconomic indicators and financial market data, provide measurable predictive signal for equity market returns and implied volatility regimes, while demonstrating comparatively limited explanatory power in sovereign yield forecasting.

Disclaimer

The directional accuracy figures above look modest and the ROC-AUC scores sit in a middle range, but by quantitative trading standards this still constitutes a real, exploitable signal, assuming trading fees, slippage, and other real-world execution frictions are ignored. Financial markets have a notoriously low signal-to-noise ratio, so an edge this size, applied consistently at scale, is meaningful rather than negligible.

Marcos López de Prado, in Advances in Financial Machine Learning, argues that models claiming very high out-of-the-box AUC (e.g. above ~0.70) on directional price forecasts are usually a red flag for data leakage or look-ahead bias, not genuine skill. Read in that light, the mid-range AUCs reported here are more plausible as a real, if modest, signal than a suspiciously "too good" one would be.

A Note on Inference-Time Sign Flips

Not every prediction will make intuitive sense in isolation. A speech read as bearish by the FED can still be followed by positive returns across multiple asset classes and horizons, and vice versa. This isn't necessarily a model failure, since markets are largely efficient, meaning most available information is already priced in before a statement becomes official. During downturns, even overtly bearish commentary can trigger a relief rally, simply because the worst was already priced in weeks earlier and any small sliver of hope gets bought. During upturns, even bullish commentary can trigger selling, since good news has already been priced in and it becomes a convenient point for profit taking.

This dynamic is consistent with Eugene Fama's Efficient Market Hypothesis and Andrew Lo's Adaptive Market Hypothesis, both of which help explain why markets are so difficult to predict from news sentiment alone. The relationship between "news tone" and "price direction" is regime-dependent rather than fixed.
